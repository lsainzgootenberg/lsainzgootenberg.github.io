A Chicago Geography of Sound

An essay with an accompanying playlist; track titles are set in italics throughout.

Midway, Scott Tuma, 2001

Searching for music is meant to be a public endeavor. It should start with hearing a distant kind of bass line, from the street below, and running downstairs to see what all the rattling is about. Sometimes if you’ve only slept a few hours, you’d believe you were descending into some kind of madness. It could just be someone’s speaker in the next apartment over. Or car tires rolling on the crackled asphalt.

On 55th Street, it’s cars the whole way down. The storefronts are conglomerated into little shopping centers, and then emptiness. Another stretch of blocks, a few more stores. Time stretches out farther along these streets, each block as its own island. On 55th, I’m looking for a place on the map that doesn’t exist anymore. Somehow, putting the address into Google Maps yields a result. 1503 E 55th Street. The red pin on Google Maps lies on top of someone’s front yard on Harper Avenue.

I watched a woman taking out the trash where Charlie Parker had his last Chicago performance. I couldn’t see her face over the shrubbery in her yard. Her windows were minuscule, and her whole life seemed contained. Each yard is the same square shape, with slight variations on the shrubs and plants and black fences circling them. Sonny Rollins, Max Roach, and Thelonious Monk were in her yard when it was the Bee Hive nightclub, before it closed in 1956.

Cherokee – Live at the Bee Hive, Clifford Brown and Max Roach, 1979

Sax and trumpet sounds have been long replaced by the quaint Hyde Park shopping center. The whole street has a dull and muted tone to it. Grey and beige bricks repeat and loop down 55th and 56th. It all looks washed out, with the exception of a few red-orange trees. The wind from the lake pushes bitterly on my face when I cycle west down 57th, so I walk instead.

When I walk past the Midway, my mind starts to play tricks on me. Blank spaces where buildings should stand appear like words cut out of sentences. There’s a wooden fence that stands on the edge of a couple of blocks. The choppiness is familiar, but entirely incoherent, with entire sections cut away and wood panels falling off of it. It doesn’t circle around the blocks completely, just covering enough to appear like an enclosure to passing cars. It holds in nothing but disparate patches of brown and green grasses. In the summers, it grows long and begins to look like a cornfield is sprouting on a city block.

Walking Chicago requires a speculative mode of looking, a kind of attention to details in negative space. The blank spaces stand in for question marks. Who might have lived here? What did the streets look like? Why did they leave? Sometimes, you even see a block with only one or two houses still standing. The lots still have fences, as if they are waiting for someone to return. The unpainted brick on the sides of houses that still stand were never supposed to be exposed, a spectral underbelly.

Mercy, Mercy, Mercy! Live at “The Club”, Cannonball Adderley Quintet, 1967

The section of 55th Street by the Beehive Lounge had a strange progression, a lifetime marked by distinctive bursts of disinvestment, intoxication, and debate. As far back as the late 19th century, it was the only place where liquor was allowed in the area, so it became the site for bars and taverns to proliferate. Persisting through prohibition, the street maintained itself as the central gathering space for the neighborhood. In the 1930s and 40s, illicit venues and speakeasies turned into established nightclubs.

These were the two decades when jazz thrived under restrictive conditions. Racial covenants created harsh boundaries between areas and limited movement. Entertainment and retail were concentrated along a few major streets west of Washington Park, all the way from Woodlawn up to the south loop. Clubs like the De Lisa and the Rhumboogie operated around the clock with a variety show format, non-stop entertainment by singers, dancers, and in-house bands. In the wave of a second Great Migration to Chicago, Black musicians like Sun Ra arrived in Chicago and found work in the nightclubs on Garfield, 55th, and 63rd.

The De Lisa closed in 1958, later reopening and then closing again. There’s no record of how it became an empty lot. In Hyde Park, the demolition of the Bee Hive and other jazz venues was a more intentional form of destruction. Almost the entire stretch of 55th Street from University to the railroad tracks on S. Lake Park Ave was demolished in a decade-long effort by the Southeast Chicago Commission to revitalize the city and start from scratch. A coalition of institutions, including the University of Chicago, created the basis for the commissions and legislature that displaced over 81,000 people across Chicago.

As the 1960s marked an unprecedented transformation of urban space for thousands of Black Chicagoans, sound was also changing radically. Earlier decades had been dominated by big-band orchestras, complete with four sections of trombonists, saxophonists, trumpeters, and percussionists. By the late 1950s, it was the age of the artist. Dancing music had given in to a new idea – listening.

While countless South Side jazz venues closed in the 1960s, an independent group in the South Side music scene was building an emerging coalition of Black musicians. It was to be called the Association for the Advancement of Creative Musicians, or the AACM. Started by Muhal Richard Abrams and Jodie Christian, it was first and foremost a space for budding jazz artists to develop their practice on their own terms, but also a place for honest community.

The AACM created a philosophy, rooting their practice first and foremost on improvisation and free thinking. The workshops and music school would provide classes for students, while more established members would mentor budding musicians. They somehow succeeded in nourishing both the artist and the collective.

The music that emerged from these intersecting projects was wildly unconventional. It zigzagged through genres, mixing bells, whistles, classical, funk, reggae, off-kilter rhythms, and unknown metal objects. Parallel to the visual art collectives of OBAC and AfriCOBRA, the AACM was interested in creating an expansive aesthetic that reflected the material knowledge and history of its members.

Like the colors and the indescribable magic of AfriCOBRA’s aesthetics, AACM’s music is rooted in the singularity and the tenacity of each artist, but undeniably formed through a persistent belief in exchange. There are echoes of different times and musical movements, but nothing is completely abstract. Bells and cymbals crash into seemingly stable rhythms as bass lines move up and down. Trills blanket over each other. Melodies are sung low and high, coming up out of nothing but a soft percussion. Brief pauses give rise to a deep sense of reflection. Forgetting genre and forgoing definition, the avant-garde of Chicago still refuses to pin itself down.

Many of the musicians who emerged out of the AACM tradition have gone on to have expansive careers in New York and Europe. Yet Chicago remained the incubator for their art. The space for their jazz was unlimited. Never housed in just one building, it travelled from one scene to the next. Free concerts and performances across the city. Free workshops for young kids and artists. Free, both in movement and in expression.

W-138 (Opus 51), Anthony Braxton, 1976

…

On the Midway Plaisance every fall, the Hyde Park Jazz Festival appears. It’s a stronghold for jazz, a tangible breathing form that appears along the small hills of the Midway and within the folding chairs and blankets. The music merges with the space. The Midway no longer looks like a road, but becomes its own block entirely. Jazz trios, trumpeters, blue saxophones, drummers, food trucks, jewelry stands, incense burning, dancing, sitting.

This space doesn’t feel temporary. The neighbors that stop by to sit, listen, or dance have been taking in jazz for decades. Some of them have heard stories from 63rd Street nightclubs or 55th Street bars. Others used to visit 504 E 75th St to hear Von Freeman and his band play at the New Apartment Lounge. Jam sessions on Tuesdays took over the space from midnight to 3 AM. Some head down to the Logan Center on Wednesday nights, and others flock to the Jazz Jams run by the Southside Jazz Coalition. These are places where sound was carried on from one person to another, a language of exchange. The music becomes a travelogue. It rifles through memories of places, or becomes a way of living on through sound itself.

Notes on the Industries of Red Hook, Brooklyn

Red Hook once moved New York. In the west, the two rivers converge into a bay. Jetting out into the harbor, the neighborhood marks the end and the beginning of waterways, a singular point of convergence that was primed for the movement of people and commerce. Ports began dotting the curved cape after the Civil War, and the construction of the Erie Basin cemented its industrial futures.

Today, the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway forms an unusual border of noise and traffic around the neighborhood. Red Hook, unlike its neighbors to the north and east, remains as an anomaly in an otherwise gentrified part of Brooklyn. There are no trains that go to Red Hook. Without the guaranteed explosion of tourists, tenants, real estate agents, Red Hook’s isolation has yielded a bizarre time capsule.

As the asphalt that covers most of Brooklyn becomes rough cobblestone, row houses with iron fences give way to a sea of factory buildings. Beyond this, masts and cranes raze the sky above the shipping buildings. In the nineteenth century, Norwegian, Puerto Rican, Italian, and Irish immigrants staked their claim to these streets, moving in and out as the city underwent demographic changes. Now, Red Hook’s warehouses, shipping facilities, and shipping ports are a case study in retrofitting. Visible changes in the neighborhood come gradually; they aren’t marked by the construction of high-rises or the calibrated scrapping of the factory buildings that line the ports. Instead, Red Hook’s changes are characterized by the reappropriation of its industrial bones. The factories have taken on new tenants. Artist studios, seaside restaurants, breweries, and the occasional superstore. In 2008, the Ikea craze of New York took full swing with the first store opening in Red Hook. Families poured into the parking lot from all over New York, and it was considered to be the kind of investment that would bring jobs and local opportunity, particularly in an area with uneven economic investment.

As sociologist Sharon Zukin remarks, longtime neighborhood businesses found themselves adapting to a new class of visitors. By the early 2000s, Red Hook food vendors, who once served food for their families and friends at soccer games, had transformed into entrepreneurs both due to an increasing demand from non-Latino foodies and through the struggle to keep up with the city government’s bureaucratic regulations. Vendors, in constant renegotiation for their right to public space, attempt to prove their permanent place through increasing their profits and appealing toward an obsession with ‘authenticity’. Turning themselves into marketable entrepreneurs on a small scale, Red Hook now symbolized the obsessive tendency to commodify the industrial and authentic.

Six years after the COVID-19 pandemic, many of Red Hook’s factories are now in the hands of investors and larger companies. Tesla and Amazon have joined the growing number of companies who house warehouses and urban fulfillment centers in the area. This unregulated urban expansion of fulfillment centers has led to an increased number of vehicle crashes, congestion, and air quality problems. Amazon vehicles clog Columbia Street and the underpass to Carroll Gardens, and have led to workplace injury rates more than triple the national average for private companies. Yet, there’s still not a coordinating group that oversees this large-scale delivery process. For communities who already suffer from poor air quality, these facilities also pose potential harm in furthering Red Hook’s pollution.

The grain silos are now abandoned, etched with the color of dark smoke. To the east, cross-shaped red brick apartment buildings go for blocks, until the blockade of the expressway pushes up against them. The Red Hook houses, which were built in the early 1930s, have separated the neighborhood between a working class side and relative newcomers – wealthy residents drawn both by the relative isolation and ample space the neighborhood provides.

Red Hook’s allure is now cemented into state law. The announcement of the 122-acre, $3.5 billion Brooklyn Marine Terminal development plan, which will span all the way across the bay, promises a reactivation of Red Hook’s previous life as an industrial powerhouse. As a modern harbor, the project hopes to expand previously used maritime routes as an alternative to truck freight to reduce congestion.

Even as the Brooklyn Marine Terminal makes these promises, the battle for public space in Red Hook has taken center stage since the project’s announcement. At a series of open forums and city council meetings, neighbors and council members repeatedly aired their pushbacks on both how the plan ignores community input, and the risks of possible displacement with the addition of luxury housing units on the waterfront. It remains to be seen if Red Hook, which has been the site of reevaluation and popular reimagination, will be pushed toward a more livable reality.

Postmodern Panic and the Semiotics of the New Right: Irony in Red ScareMass Media in Semiotic Perspective · December 2024

A chirpy, upbeat blonde reporter makes her way through a crowd with a microphone, suddenly approaching a young woman wearing a Sailor Moon costume. “Hi! Are you a fan of Bernie Sanders?” the blond woman proceeds to ask her a series of questions on why she supports him, to which she responds, between laughing, “Because he’s a socialist.” The woman, later dubbed “Sailor Socialist” on social media platforms, became an online sensation of the left through embodying an unbothered and ironic affect that served as a counterpoint to the right-wing reporter’s attempts to enthusiastically ‘trap’ her in contradiction.

The “Sailor Socialist” in the video is none other than Dasha Nekrasova, a cultural critic, writer, and actress who co-hosts the “Red Scare” podcast with her friend Anna Khachiyan. The podcast epitomized the appeal of “Dirtbag Leftists”, which was an emerging movement in the mid 2010s that rejected both the direct and casual cultural commentary of right wing media and the passivity and civility of the liberal media. The movement took shape after the publication of Amber A’Lee Frost’s essay, The Necessity of Political Vulgarity, in Current Affairs magazine in 2016. A’Lee Frost argues for a return to political vulgarity in order to effectively combat elitism and create a new majority ideology of class consciousness. She writes, “Vulgarity is the language of the people, and so it should be among the grammars of the left, just as it has been historically, to wield righteously against the corrupt and the powerful.” Against the normalized civility of the liberal establishment, and the galvanizing vulgarity within the emerging ideology of Trumpism, A’lee Frost argued that vulgarity on the left had the potential to subvert elites and unite a new majority. Building off of such ideologies of vulgarity, Dasha and Anna’s Red Scare podcast became one of the most popular and divisive examples of the “Dirtbag Left”, and continues to create a complex counterpublic that is highly rooted in its contentious use of form and language.

Vulgarity and other similar forms of linguistic parody and appropriation thus maximize the possibility for intertextual gaps between genre, speech, and written language, creating the potential for new imagined communities. (Briggs & Bauman,150) Briggs and Bauman argue that intertextuality indexes a relationship between a text (spoken or written) and previous texts or discourse, and plays a fundamental role in shaping the function and form of a given discourse, while also shaping ideology(147). Yet, widening the intertextual gap also creates a larger opposition between signifier and signified, often transcending the limits of credibility.(Briggs & Bauman 151). Being a spoken discourse, a podcast such as Red Scare provides the ideal medium for creating linguistic intertextual gaps, because of its casual and ambiguous structure. Spoken language further fleshes out the emotional form of discourse, and indexes toward different social meanings, genres, and publics. On a purely denotational level beyond the affect and tone of the text itself, Red Scare creates a string of dialogue that intertextually relates and disavows their own talking points, popular lexicon, and mass media into one contradictory dialogue, achieving an ambiguous tone of ironic mockery against, seemingly, almost everything.

Thus, through Dasha and Anna’s flirtation with form, they declare a refusal to be pinned down to one moral code or political ideology, by which they constitute and express an alternative epistemology of modern political affect. Red Scare’s most frequently parodied subject matter is “identity politics” riddled liberals, which they suggest operate on a strict mode of policing language and tone, rather than their optimistic purpose of democratizing experience for marginalized groups. They pose these digressions with such an ironic tone that it becomes hard to discern what they believe from what they are mocking. Increasingly, their vulgarity and humor has been repositioned against their original “dirtbag left” ideals. No longer reacting against elite, classist establishment politics and ideas, the two are carving out an online medium that is no longer just leftist nihilism, but verges into a right-wing sphere of vitriol.

Post-ironic thinking has been almost synonymous with the medium of the internet, creating epistemological bubbles of extremism that are both covert and direct. Inviting outrage through offensive language is one of the primary ways that online publics operate– sincerity has become passé and cringe, and the new modality of ‘post-ironic’ humor rules over everything, creating a confusing opening for extremism. The anonymity of the medium, as well as the sheer amount of platforms, information, and content, ignited new bubbles of right-wing extremism that are highly reliant on parody and humor. Those with extremist views are able to regularly express their beliefs by operating under a tone of complete irony and joke, by indexing to a humorous intent, which camouflages or protects them from a reader’s interpretation of those beliefs as sincere.

This paper provides an analysis of humor and irony as particular features of “new right” ideology, particularly through the casual spoken language that is a key feature of the Red Scare podcast. I posit that their spoken irony surpasses traditional usages of irony, creating an even larger intertextual gap between the signifier and the signified. Their destabilized and ironic tone functions to mock the moral and social stability of establishment politics, making their crass tone an end in itself. They construct such a tone through both denotatively explicit methods and some more implicit methods. First, through their spoken tone and affect, permitted by the casual medium of the podcast, they create an intertextual gap between their content and their affect. Structurally, they directly recontextualize mass media events and theory into their casual and vulgar tone, indexing a humorous reconsideration of the political into the nonpolitical. This constant lexical indexing to political thinkers, texts, and internet media creates a limited public sphere that is inherently exclusionary. Through their highly ironic tonal shifts, they also reflexively index to create a stylized private-public persona that encourages an aesthetic homogeneity of non-ideological purity, while discouraging individual self-expression. This vulgarity, however, cannot function in opposition or toward one political ideology, because it appears to point in all directions. Their speech, therefore, no longer conveys direct meaning through its content, but rather through its form. Through such a reconstruction of form over content, they are able to both resist critique and unintentionally assume a new status as figures of an anti-ideological counterpublic that operates beyond a concrete belief system. This paper does not aim to uncover a concrete genealogy of Red Scare’s own political ideologies, or even how these viewpoints have shifted since 2018. Rather, it looks at the various layers of contradiction and irony within Red Scare’s speech and affect, to see how they devalue coherence through content and repeatedly play with style and form instead.

To semiotically analyze these complex patterns of irony, humor, and performed distance, we can first look to the most basic element. In its most basic form, there exist two elements of any sign: the signifier and the signified. The signifier is the sign’s most basic, physical form, or what it looks like. The signified is what the sign denotes (Cobley, 12). This relationship is subjective and contextually dependent. In sincerity, the denotational meaning is the message, creating little distance between the speaker and the recipient. In traditional irony, there are two different levels of meaning, a distance between what is being said and what is meant, essentially, the denotational meaning and its connotational meaning diverging. Red Scare irony is slightly more complex, because it operates on a third layer. On Red Scare, there is an increasing and unusual unity of what is being said and what is meant, while the irony is expressed in self-denial of sincerity, effectively creating a post-ironic affect where nothing can be taken at face value. This first example, from an episode titled Taliban Mindset, exhibits a few key elements of their linguistic and tonal style.

Dasha: I declared a Jihad against body fat today at the gym and entered what I’d like to call a kind of a Taliban mindset. I was inspired to work out really diligently and with incredible focus as if I had a Jihad of my own. So I thank them for that. But as podcasters, we are kind of on the front lines of podcasting, of culture.

Anna: Yeah.[laughter] the front lines of podcasts.

Dasha: And people should be thanking us for our service instead of mocking us and making fun of us on the internet all day.

Anna: Yeah, we did a LOT of research for this episode. Yeah.

Dasha: We kind of did. Yeah.

Anna: I mean, my take is that this is like an opioid war. Yeah. That’s my Alex Jones galaxy brain take. And the market has shifted. Yeah. And Afghanistan, you know, I think it’s like kind of like an autoimmune thing. It’s like geopolitical iatrogenics. Like you got to keep the war machine and like the big pharma machine going at all times. So you have to branch out into new theaters.

Dasha: Because fentanyl is the new, I guess, Opioid.

(Taliban Mindset, August 17, 2021)

In this dialogue, representative of typical Red Scare-isms, we see how Dasha and Anna reflexively position themselves in opposition to sincerity, invoking a rhetoric that appropriates the seriousness of war (“thanking us for our service”, “front lines of podcasts”), juxtaposing such a language against a casual dialogue between two friends. Dasha quickly indexes toward a mass of critics, and Anna follows this by a statement affirming their thorough research(which is credible when looking at the scope of the episode as a whole), but disavows this sincerity with her ironic and exaggerated tone. Anna returns to the general discussion of Afghanistan, but provides a “take” that is highly aware of its absurdity, referencing far-right pundit Alex Jones to make fun of his tendency toward conspiracy theories, and using a lexicon that indexes a leftist ideology (war machine and big pharma machine). The statement as a whole critiques how the war in Afghanistan is perpetuated by the same destructive structures of power and greed as the Opioid crisis, and how its unintended consequences of harm are just another way that the U.S. can covertly continue exploitative cycles. Yet, the hyperbolic tone of the statement, as well as the laughter, indicates even further distance from this highly ironic take.

While Dasha’s statement of her “Jihad” against body-fat does not mean the direct opposite, the recontextualization of “jihad” within an everyday statement creates an uncertain distance within the sincerity of the statement. The denotational meaning of the word Jihad is struggle in Arabic, but its referential associations for Anna and Dasha are clearly with Islamic extremism, given the context of the surrounding conversation about the Taliban regaining power in August of 2021. Yet, a third meaning, constructed from the interpreter’s context (Anna and Dasha as white, female New Yorkers who build Ikea furniture and go to the gym), is what provides this statement its ironic meaning– the inherent distance between the Red Scare Podcasters and “declaring Jihad”. As shown, this ambiguity once worked to critique aspects of mainstream liberal culture, but as the lines blur between irony, absurdity, and sincerity, they place themselves in contrast to every ideology and belief system, as a part of their new ethos against all coherence.

Such intertextual gaps are further complicated through Red Scare’s casual structure, which cultivates a conversational and humorous format through the medium of the podcast, but diverges indexically from their position as cultural commentators with advanced understandings of theory and philosophy. Episodes do not have a consistent length, but usually end up being over an hour. A familiar excerpt of All The Things She Said by the early 2000s Russian pop duo t.A.T.u. plays to signify the beginning of an episode. From this, we gain an understanding of their positionality– they operate as ‘it girls’ within a subsection of New York art critics and know a wide social circle of writers, artists, and actors. They constantly reflexively position themselves as “girls”, indexing a casual and relatable attitude toward their own opinions. Yet, the content strays from this casual position by revealing their obvious understanding of intellectual life and culture, as they do thorough research for each episode, and pull in casual references to cultural theorists and political thinkers. This, inherently, is a performance that they understand creates further incongruity, speaking to an intertextuality of genre. In this sense, Dasha and Anna are aware that this intertextual gap widens the scope of possible listeners by appealing to an audience that lies outside a purely intellectual circle.

Beyond just their structure, they de-emphasize the directness of their language and subvert ‘authenticity’, as a way of contrasting themselves from mass media radio platforms and news outlets. (Bauman and Briggs 2003, Kunreuther 2010) Following Kunreuther’s assertion that directness and transparency are ideological functions of neoliberal development on FM radio in Nepal, Red Scare becomes the ideal subject of the opposite function. While the casual setting and language of their conversations do create a “temporal co-presence” (Kunreuther, 2010), they refuse a voice or a language that indexes directness and authenticity. Key to this tone is their consistent ‘vocal fry’, which is an emerging quality of women’s speech across the United States. They push the ends of their sentences into a lower register than their actual voice while speaking slowly and dryly. This cadence, which is often associated with an unprofessionalism and boredom, or with celebrities like Kim Kardashian and Julia Fox, is overtly contrasted by Red Scare’s understandings of philosophy, literature, and critical theory. The incongruence between their linguistic tone and content is an explicit rejection of the direct and calming lexicon and speech often associated with ‘liberal’ news outlets and sources, but also the more crass and emotional speech patterns associated with “rightwing” news outlets.

Speech not only indexes certain social and economic backgrounds, but also indexes emotion(Briggs & Bauman, 158). The Red Scare “vocal fry” creates an emotionally flat and dry sense of speech, which effectively equalizes all of their content to lacking a high emotional quality. Thus, when the Red Scare girls discuss geopolitical conflicts, it has the same resonance as when they discuss the New York art scene. The only emotional resonance that really emerges out of this flatness is laughter, which cuts through perceived attempts at sincerity. This indexes a kind of emotional indifference, which is once again contradicted by the extremely long episode lengths, which go over the same topics repeatedly. This indirect quality of speech only further suggests an ideological apathy that indexes both toward and against any political statement.

By placing internet lexicon and phrases alongside serious stories from the news cycle, Red Scare indexes not only a disillusionment with ‘woke’ politics and liberal ideology, but mass media as a whole. Their casual tonality is overtly contrasted by the content of their conversations– by oscillating between everyday ‘girl’ topics, pop culture, politics, and their highly knowledgeable background in theory, they index toward the absurdity of modern epistemologies, by creating their own alternative force of confusion. As with the title of the podcast, Red Scare, the hosts frequently use intertextuality to ironize the seriousness of the new cycle, and even to ironize their own relation to such media. Their episode titles, which index to listeners what the episode will cover, play with this duality of serious and unserious. Often, these titles stitch two different references together, through altering a phrase by recontextualizing another word within it. A few names for episodes include: You’ve Gaza Be Kidding Me, Hoezempic, Gay December, Handmaids Fail, Non Fungible Podcast. From a standpoint of pure lexicon, Red Scare’s language appears accessible and casual, but their constant indexing and recontextualization of highly specific internet moments and terms creates an exclusionary counter-public audience, one which is reflexively indexing toward their own identity. Straightforwardly using casual internet language and short sentences, they scatter and recontextualize internet slang within their larger analysis, while referring back to anecdotes and personal experiences. In the case of the “Taliban Mindset” excerpt, a listener must have a thorough understanding of both “Alex Jones galaxy brain” and “Geopolitical Iatrogenics” to even begin to interpret the ironic reading. Through using words like “LARP”, “CHUD”, “Trad”, “Sigma”, and “Blackpilled”, they restrict the scope of their audience, indexing a desire for a reflexive audience of listeners similar to themselves.

The underlying structural component behind the entire podcast is how it recaps the news and reads articles from across the media spectrum, creating a subversion of the circulative “bubbles” of divided communication. Yet, this exercise in recontextualization challenges the perceived divisions between such ideologies by agglomerating them into one mockable aggregate. Even before their ‘turn’ toward the new right, Red Scare toyed around with the genre of the bubble by referencing people and articles across the political aisle and by being open to having a wide variety of guests on the show. While media references across a full political spectrum get airtime on Red Scare, what unites them is their universal fuel to the fire of Red Scare humor.

Anna: It’s so insane. I’m in a group chat where we were discussing the Dean essay and somebody sent, like, a wall text from some art opening that’s called “Breathe Toward Climate and Social Justice.” And this is the first sentence. “The confluence of cataclysmic events that mark the year 2020 among them, the global COVID-19 pandemic and ensuing economic crisis, the rise in anti-Asian hate crimes–

Dasha: [screams and laughter] Yeah, that’s insane. The anti Asian hate crimes because they tried to say it was because Trump said Kung flu virus that was causing Haitian guys to push old Asian ladies on the subway track. (Fake and Gaetz, November 21, 2024. )

Yet, in spite of this universal affect of mockery, the Red Scare podcast’s popularity requires the anonymous communities and participation of both critics and fans. While Red Scare operates through a one-way system of production and release onto podcast platforms, both Dasha and Anna are hyper aware of the online communication spheres that their discourse creates, amongst liberals, leftists, and conservatives alike. Yet, as opposed to the circulative scope of “socially charged life” created by linguistic communications on Zambian radio articulated by Debra Spitulnik, the podcast creates an internal feedback loop of indexical references. The aggregate archetype of “The Lib” is fashioned as a rhetorical device to mock those with mainstream politics, becoming an essential phrase in their language. In the episode Taliban Mindset, Dasha proclaims, “This situation regardless of what you think… It’s sad… It brings out your inner libtard.” In an episode titled, “You’ve Gaza be Kidding Me, the “Lib” comes up several times. Anna says, “I mean, at all, the free speech libtard in me says that no one should be fired for expressing their political beliefs.” (You’ve Gaza Be Kidding Me, October 13, 2023) In both instances they direct identification with this figure, signally a break out of their ironic tone into sincerity, but then using internet lexicon to disavow this relationship, all within the course of one sentence. While they do make fun of some right wing figures, these critiques rarely amount to an aggregate character of “the right”, preferring to keep an ambiguous line of support. In this sense, both the liberal and conservative provide a central part of their identity and their relation to viewers.

Engaging in such a constant tone of dismissiveness and irony toward both critics and fans creates a kind of metapopularity, by which they are immune to any humiliation as public and private figures. While they make around $48,000 a month from just their Patreon, they appear to also be wildly unpopular, as the primary subject of several think pieces and articles in major media outlets. How can this contradiction be explained? By embracing all criticism in a tone of metairony, they confusingly disavow and embrace any criticism. In an episode titled Vanity Unfair, the girls review an article about them published in Vanity Fair titled, Inside the New Right, Where Peter Thiel Is Placing His Biggest Bets.

Dasha: “But then when I actually did read the article today he makes mention of how the people on the “new right” always feel like they’re under attack. And I was like, okay not no, yeah. In the first excerpt, Dasha acknowledges some truths within the article. The double negative of “not no” places a casual distance between the statement and her response, and then she quickly widens the intertextual gap by signalling the blame toward “the media”. Yet, this is also said in a spoken ironic tone, through repeatedly indexing toward language of extreme punishment, and the higher register of her voice that is adopted for a portion of the sentence. This is further complicated by the context of the episode, Vanity Unfair, in which the entire structure pertains to the publication of an article criticizing them, which indexes Red Scare’s sincere assertion and concern that mass media is constantly attacking them. In this way, they resist a reaction that makes them seem volatile and exposed, preferring to distance themselves from even their own defenses.

In spite of such a distance from both fans and critics, Anna and Dasha create a conjoined public and private body through exaggerating and ironizing both. Their utilization of the medium itself creates a potency for the unification of both ‘selves’ – by merging themselves, and in particular, images and words that remark upon their own bodies, they iconize their public status through externalizing the white, female body. In the same episode, Vanity Unfair, the two indexically reference their personal lives.

Anna: We woke the baby up trying to play a clip off my phone. Can Peter Thiel get us a wet nurse up in here?” (Vanity Unfair, April 26, 2022)

While they mock the assertion that Peter Thiel, the right-wing crypto-billionaire with fascistic tendencies, is “funding” them, they do not directly deny it. Rather, they redirect listeners’ attention toward an alternative reading: There is no way that they would be capable of even doing such a thing, because they are just ‘dumb girls’ who do messy things, just like everyone else. In redirecting their critique toward themselves, and particularly themselves as private figures (Anna being the mother of a young child) they create an ironic form of self-deprecation, yet, this statement cannot be taken for its direct, denotational reading, as they are highly aware of their intelligence.

Beyond this, a central part of their successful use of irony arises from the intertextual gap between the references to their bodies and the extremism they employ. Particularly in their personal style and online posting of ‘thirst traps’, they have attracted immense controversy. In 2021, they released a new Red Scare merch featuring what appeared to be an index toward the ISIS flag. They advertised the T-shirts through posing seductively in a series of mirror selfies on their website. This is just one amongst many examples of their style, which fuses their public image of ironic ideology and sexualized images: Dasha poses seductively with a mini skirt and an assault rifle and dons a recontextualized red MAGA hat that reads “See you in Hell”. Anna and Dasha are seen posing with Alex Jones, and Dasha is seen aiming a gun at a target with a Palestinian Keffiyeh. While their intent was to create an ambiguous reading to stir up controversy, it is also clear that this ‘irony’ functions due to their whiteness and thinness. Their own bodies index an intertextual distance between the denotational reading of extremist symbolism, but also point out the contradictions of this intertextual gap, a clear reminder that their primary audience is as reflexive as it can be: unmarked, educated, thin white people.

Such an iconized reflexive image creates a stylistic appeal toward irony that is immune to humiliation. Unlike Michael Warner’s reading, which asserts a certain instability in the public figures “phallic image”, in which celebrities bodies are “prostheses for our own mutant desirability” (395), Dasha and Anna publicly control the attention toward the image of their bodies in order to subvert such a public ritual of display. As Warner asserts that Reagan’s body has no private subject behind it, because he makes no claim to his “phallic image”, neither do the bodies of the Red Scare girls, but for the opposing reason. Within their language they index toward a stylized reflexive character of themselves, while in constant reference to the humiliation of the non-white, thin body. Unlike the separation of voice from body in traditional FM radio broadcasting, they purposefully reference and index their thinness and whiteness. Even in saying, “I declared a Jihad against body fat at the gym”, Dasha indexes a casually extreme relationship to her own body, indexing a kind of self discipline and a mental image of the podcast host in a listener’s imagination. This further exemplifies how they construct a counterpublic of fans amongst primarily white women. To construct their own mass audiences, Dasha and Anna ironically praise the bodies of their fanbase, parodying and embodying the public body’s inevitable display. By constantly indexing toward their own thinness and ideal body type, they exile and marginalize the ‘other’ outside of the Red Scare counterpublic. With the self-awareness that they will be put on display, they redirect the power toward displaying themselves.

Within their dialogues of intertextuality and reference, Anna and Dasha almost never directly address or acknowledge a personal relationship to their fans, instead creating a relationship with their audience that encourages a homogenous position of performing oneself in total alignment with the hosts. In their special episodes known as “Loveline”, the ladies of the pod solicit questions about love and sex from their audience. (Eclipse Loveline, April 8, 2024)

In contrast to the model of the ‘neoliberal’ radio tone discussed by Kunreuther, Red Scare promotes a kind of individualism that negates self-love, action, and even freedom. The lack of direct conversation between these pre-recorded questions and the actual hosts further concretizes a separation between the fans and the hosts, but draws in each subject through the very desire for the individual to fulfill a goal of ‘personal transformation’ that conforms wholly to the expectations of Anna and Dasha. Rooted in such an inaccessibility, it disavows an internal subjective voice. Young women do not ask themselves, “what can I do?”, but rather, “what would Dasha and Anna do?”

Through this reconstitution of form over content, Red Scare is embodying a new kind of public figure of “anti-ideology” who compels and attracts a metapopularity through their construction of an ironic private-public merging. The orientation and mode of their language not only infuriates audiences through its content, but particularly through its unintelligible form of operation. Yet, the potential for such a vulgarity to be directed toward a singular ideological statement is impeded by its ironic tone, as their performance of intertextuality points absurdly in every direction, creating an essentialist anti-ideology. Briggs and Bauman emphasize the two-way relationship between intertextuality and ideology, arguing that, “Broader social, cultural, and ideological, and political-economic formations shape and empower intertextual strategies and the manner in which ideologies of intertextuality and their associated practices shape society and history.” (160) This dialectical relationship suggests that the Red Scare girls are creating an emerging non-political movement that is inherently self-oriented, emerging from their own nihilistic beginnings as “Dirtbag Leftists”, but now becoming an entirely self-encompassed movement. Strangely, this suggests that Red Scare is creating a new ‘identity politics’ through their outright disavowal of identity politics. While Anna and Dasha call out identity politics for its obsession with creating strict, moral boundaries and concretizing categories of identity, their increasing obsession against aggregate categories such as “elites” and “woke”, as well as the nonsensical indexicality toward names and references in their speech, creates a kind of non-ideology steeped in essentialist identity politics.

While such a counterpublic of contrarian ideals appears too contradictory to build itself outside online pockets of intertextual extremes, it seems to have succeeded in its creation of a stylish class of left and right-wing political digression, but not through apathy, rather, through a reinvention of the self. This ‘self’ is rooted not in total absurdism or liberation from boxes of neoliberal individualism, but instead replaces this with an aesthetic expectation that simply reinforces hegemonic structures of the self– Catholic, thin, and “traditional”, incorporating slurs and offensive language into casual speech, and disavowing all things woke. In order for these new elites to function beyond their original medium of the internet, they operate under a stylish reimagining of a self-abrasive search for disobedience, by which they can hardly look beyond their own reflection. To counteract this requires a commitment towards both content and form, toward political action and against apathy. Until then, our ears might be subjected to hearing “Ozempic”, “JD Vance”, and “LARPing” in one incoherent rambling.

Trapped in-between

Looking back at the portion of selected text more explicitly, a certain pessimism emerges from Benjamin. A medium that holds the potential to change our sense-perception is equally or perhaps even more tied to the commodification of the cultural object. Is this dialectic still the challenge of our time? Trapped between the camera’s potential for altering our sense perception, therefore pluralizing our ways of seeing, versus the production of photographs and mass artwork as a commodity fetish and an aestheticization of itself, we still struggle to find the vocabulary to deal with this problem. If we understand reality to be a photograph, however, we are struggling to understand the dynamic motion of reality, one which the ‘realness’ and the ‘truth’ of a photograph cannot really grasp.

Art critics sometimes disavow brand new technologies in favor of the allure of the past, and the media made for masses is relegated to a status of unsavory and trashy, until some time has passed, and it has moved to the realm of nostalgia or trend. There is then the problem of recognition and of choice. There are no longer 2 channels of TV. Images are unavoidable. Images live replicated on our clothing, our computers, buses, and schools. Images are categorized and judged in as quickly as a few seconds. They are relevant, irrelevant, beautiful, horrifying, ironic, sad, boring, strange. These meanings are never static. Something gauche becomes desirable, as soon as a high social class imbues it with a sense of cultural capital and nostalgia.

The experience of technological newness, however, is often a sensorial overload. We have not yet adjusted ourselves to the physical undertaking of a new organ of perception, a new means to understand it. I find that art is beginning to grapple with this sensory overwhelm– but not by effacing it or denying it, but by swimming through it, by creating a perverse pleasure or disgust in its extreme superimpositions. New kinds of music are constructed through mixing subgenre with subgenre, already heavily mediated by electronics. Remixes speed up, videos melt into pixels or slop, people parody distraction to a point of perversity. These are built out of the logic of assemblage, an associative dialectic of disconnected pieces that are montaged together into a moving sculpture of distractions. Sometimes, they function by linking older forms of ‘distraction’ or ‘artwork’ to contemporary ones(Hegel juxtaposed with the fashion show).

Playing with the sensorial overload: An active performance through three forms of ‘distraction’: a fashion show, a game of subway surfers, another mobile game, and the first chapter of Hegel’s Phenomenology of Spirit. Photo by Léa, 2023.

This isn’t an attempt to link a latent, persistent kind of ritual of first technology to some modern facet of technological production. It is a lesson in contrast, a note in the absurd, one which often points out the structure and the destruction of the medium.

The problem of using art to confront reality continues as one of reification. It is the lag between the base and the superstructure. As a new technology( or use value of an existing technology) emerges, it is both feared and desired. It takes a while to be distributed to a mass audience, and to be popularly understood as generative. It tows a line between obscurity, danger, and excitement. It is only once we find ourselves adjusted to the tempos of a new thing, that we begin to question its existence or reason in the first place.

But when an art form begins to lose its expected value, or, in Benjamin’s terms, its aura, what kind of meaning can it have? Benjamin struggles through this generative potential, because the mass of images alters our social perception of artwork. It is no longer a question of truth or reality, but a question of its political power. We don’t remember the moments in which we are photographed. We remember it through a strange jumble of senses, feelings, colors, and linkages. As for artfulness, the photographer who, as Benjamin says, attempts to find an “interesting juxtaposition” (293, Little History of Photography), is essentially attaching an associative meaning onto the image after it has been taken. The meaning is connected to an established aesthetic rule or a visual coincidence, not a concrete experience.

As the meaning of artwork often occurs in a space beyond words or language, it takes time for the unconscious mind to bring out a description. The art world and the political world have this in common: they operate in spectacle, but also in reflexivity. They are constantly trying to consider what they are, and what they have been en masse. The problem continues to be the endless lag, the assessment of cultural production and economic production.

I link my perception of media to these moments, which I wove from their respective times into my memory, taking on a new character and developing meaning and magic.

The adaptability of history

If the cinematic camera is like an alien mode of perception, which has the potential to slow down or speed up time, how might it reimagine our historical selves? Evidently, the largest change came with colonialism. The collected stories, written down in the early colonial period in the Mayan highlands, are referred to as an “instrument for seeing clearly.” It is thus the form and structure of the stories that remains the guide, adapting to the crisis of meaning.

The same seems to be the case for the moving image. The lessons of the object lie in its structure, the way it insists on re-creation instead of linearity. It is as if you are viewing a historical transformation in real time, from a flat landscape of image, to a dynamic, shocking collection of movement, a vision from another world.

Explication on the Glass Essay by Anne Carson

It is hard to choose a singular element, technique, or even repeated image to focus on with Anne Carson’s The Glass Essay, simply because of her haunting ability to completely dissolve boundaries from one place, time, or feeling to another. I began to get the sense that we ourselves as readers are on a moor of sorts, slipping between the paralyzed ice, and the “gold milk” of light on the landscape(18), navigating through Carson’s narrative of inertia and her continuous movement between subjects in an attempt to face herself.

Both Carson and Brontë cling to an illegibility in their pain, but find a liberatory comfort in the repetition of actions which become the main mode of spiritual reflection. Carson points to how Emily’s condition of confinement and despair was dismissed by her critics and biographers. Carson, too, finds herself overexamined by her own critics, namely her mother and Dr. Haw. “Why hold onto all that?”, says her mother, to which she responds, “Where can I put it down?” (4) The response of “where” attests to how physically embodied and dispersed her grief is. She lives through it by repeating the same actions in only a few settings, finding herself in her mother’s kitchen staring out of the window, traversing the folds of the frozen and muddy moor itself, or even in Law’s room and bed. As Carson sits in the kitchen, or walks the landscape, Emily, too, becomes embodied in her strange and undefined routines: “She spent/most of the hours of her life brushing the carpet, walking the moor/or whaching.” (6) Through her extended time spent with Emily, Carson is able to speculatively redetermine and trace her “little raw soul” (6), which finds itself in a space between wonder and closed-off despair. This condition is dual pronged: functionally a kind of prison, but so beyond reality that it is almost liberatory.

We find the same slippery relationship on the moors themselves, which are the primary site for Carson’s own “whaching”. The second mention of the moors highlights this ambivalence directly: “My lonely life around me like a moor/my ungainly body stumping over the mud flats with a look of/transformation/that dies when I come in the kitchen door.” (2) Her loneliness, notably separate from herself, manifests itself in the mudflats of the moorlands, but the place is also the site of a definite transformation, an unbridled kind of emotion that is contrastingly stifled by the kitchen.

The kitchen is where Carson’s self is buried behind the grating and brief diatribes of her mother, where she finds herself “lowered into an atmosphere of glass”. (2) Glass, sharp and painful, is also a material of complete visibility, an echoing of her Mother’s harsh voice of reason. But glass also takes the softer form of the window, which serves to mediate and reflect the moor onto the space inside. “Out the window I can see dead leaves ticking over the flatland…At the middle of the moor/where the ground goes down into a depression,/the ice has begun to unclench./Black open water comes/curdling up like anger./ My mother speaks suddenly.” (3) This enjambment which places “curling up like anger” and the interjection of the mother in the same line allows us to traverse seamlessly between the scene beyond the window and the actions inside, the anger mediating both the landscape and the dialogue. The ‘atmosphere of glass’ on the inside is mirrored through the external frozen landscape, prone to warming and changing, an inconsistent kind of early spring that is equally frustrating as it is hopeful.

I kept coming back to the moor as the physical anchor for the entire piece– the descriptions of striding across the moorlands are both peripheral to the visions that whaching provokes, but become the material and structural basis for Carson and Emily’s inevitable and unraveling relationships. They use these material and sensorial feelings– cold, heat, frozenness, toughness– as touchstones for their acts of whaching and seeing the soul. Carson describes Emily’s ‘angry education’, writing, “Whaching a north wind grind the moor/that surrounded her father’s house on every side/formed of a kind of rock called millstone grit/taught Emily all she knew about love and its necessities—” (10) Moments later, Carson finds herself on the same millstone grit during sex with Law: “Soul is the place, stretched like a surface of millstone grit between body and mind, where such necessity grinds itself out.” (12) A coarse sedimentary rock, the soul appears briefly here as an impossibly thin layer somewhere amongst the physicality of the body and the strength of the mind. It is inseparable from the other layers, but visible somewhere as the site of love (necessity). The millstone grit rock is opaque and rough, dulling itself through time, an entirely opposing mode to Carson’s tendency to evoke sharp, transparent glass. Love cannot be sharp or entirely visible, but is as inevitable and painful as grinding down stone, as rough as “two animals gnawing and craving through one another…” (17). Love’s opacity, as opposed to her condition of transparency that arises from her frozen walks on the landscape, is not meant to be revealed in full. She writes, “Mornings when I meditated/I was presented with a nude glimpse of my lone soul/not the complex mysteries of love and hate.” The nudes are clear, frozen images that exist entirely outside of love. They function as a kind of mirrored glass, shockingly sharp in her mind, but entirely free from the millstone layers of rock, mediated instead by the north wind on the moor (9).

Still, the moor does not mean freedom by any means for Carson. This is still the place where her physical senses dip into the past involuntarily, keeping her boundlessly attached to a past. She writes, “Well there are many ways of being held prisoner,/ I am thinking as I stride over the moor.” (7). Traversing these landscapes becomes an invisible enclosure, but is also her only form of movement. I think of one description of the moor, where liberty and imprisonment conjoin into one image. “Thousands of hair-thin cracks catching the white of the light/like a jailed face/catching grins through the bars.” (18)

“We are slaves to The Herd”: Blanchette’s Herding Species reimagined through the Ecosocialism of Kohei Saito

Our inorganic world is built from discrete layers of organic material. We construct this new world from nature, and our interactions with the external world, through labor itself, are what make us human beings. In Marx’s words, “nature is man’s inorganic body”(Marx, 1844). Performances and forms of human labor, however seemingly separate from nature, are always mediated by nature itself. Yet, the human being’s “inorganic body” has been distorted by the alienated forms of labor. As capital increasingly relies on nature to expend resources and transform them into value, human existence itself is threatened. In Karl Marx’s Ecosocialism, Kohei Saito argues that this reveals a fundamental contradiction in the capitalist system– the distortion of the metabolic relationship between humans and nature threatens the ecosystem and human civilization itself, externalizing the limits to the capitalist mode of production.

Saito begins his analysis through alienation, not metabolism, and through this line of reasoning, executes an investigation as to how capitalism alters human sociality and autonomy. Alex Blanchette’s Herding Species, which ethnographically considers factory farming and the industrial pig in the Great Plains region, provides an excellent juncture to investigate what Saito’s ecological reading of Marx looks like in practice. His research builds upon these metabolic relationships and interplays between humanity and non-human, positing that the modern industrial farm fundamentally alters the hierarchical position of human workers, placing them in the porcine world itself. Blanchette’s assessment of the industrial pig complex can be assessed through Saito’s textual re-analysis of Marx, and provides further insight into how metabolism is being further mutated against its original form.

The circulation and production of the pig itself requires an analysis of how a living and dying commodity is abstracted and reified. Saito’s attention towards the contradictions of value and private labor are especially important in the case of Blanchette’s Herding Species. To use Saito’s terminology, Blanchette describes how the pig as an animal is reified and abstracted by the producer to become a unit of life, rather than a living being. This intentional reevaluation of the animal further alienates the worker from their work. Blanchette describes one instance in which a manager describes how farmers used to approach their work. They once built connections with animals and evaluated their production on an individual basis, but now, they view the pig as a component of The Herd, articulated and managed through lists, statistics, and numbers on the entire breeding stock. (Blanchette, 2015, 650) He writes, “The herd is an organizational device that helps them work on the totality of the species.” (Blanchette, 2015, 649) This emerging grouping of The Herd and the abstraction of its value further alienates human beings from species being, and mediates social class through this abstracted form of life. Blanchette’s interviews with managers highlight a central distinction that they themselves make. He explains, “We work on the Herd,” they would claim, “while hourly employees and farm managers work with the Herd.” Migrant workers thus occupy a different sociality than the manager, inching closer towards the physicality of the animal, yet, still alienated from it. Further, the factory farm is built upon solely private labor, meaning that it does not possess immediate social character, relying on a system of communications between private producers who determine the value(Saito, 2017, 105). As Saito describes it, Marx recalls how the feudal system’s division of labor still allowed serfs to maintain a relationship to the land, and often have some means of collective ownership over the land they worked. In this case, although workers often still interact with a living creature, they are conditioned to reconsider the animal as nothing more than a part of The Herd.

The emergence of disease in swine populations in the Great Plain regions revealed the largest contradictions in the factory farming system. The production and circulation of capital relies upon a continuous flow of raw materials, and capital is endlessly racing to outrun this contradiction through innovation. In the case of Blanchette’s industrial pig, this comes through the regulation of disease by means of biosecurity. The first appearance of PEDv in pigs in the Great Plains region decimated the population, and farmers sought ways to control and minimize the damage to their mode of production(Blanchette, 2015, 640). Saito’s explanation of circulating capital and production leads him to the very conclusion of Blanchette’s piece– that the repeated action of circulating capital has its limits– nature and raw materials that capital relies on are not infinite nor expendable resources. The contradiction lies in the fact that capital is constantly developing new innovations to deal with the very problems that capitalism creates– it exploits the land until it is no longer liveable, and then tries to develop new technologies to overcome its own limits. (Saito, 2017, 96) But in the case of the industrial pig, this destruction of the metabolic relationship goes beyond technological innovation. The industrial farm has innovated a new hierarchy of socialization and human behavior. Blanchette unravels a fundamental reordering to the system of production, arguing that the production of the industrial pig not only alters and restricts human sociality, but how the concern over the vitality of ‘The Herd’ places humans into the world of the pigs.

The porcine species is more than just a natural resource that is expended to create surplus value, rather, it becomes a dominating biopower that is utilized to fundamentally alienate and alter the behavior and the status of workers to a less-than-human. In contrast to Saito, humans themselves appear to be the limit to maximizing production. Saito’s argument revolves around the fact that nature is perceived to be the limiting factor for capital, but the production of pork on the Great Plains proves to go beyond this. The development of The Herd as a species category alters not only the self-perception of the worker, but also their abstract hierarchical relationship to the producer and the swine themselves. This relationship, curiously, is one which has become self-regulated. Blanchette describes the behaviors and body language of the workers, explaining how Dover farms normalizes and institutionalizes performances of biosecurity. When speaking to Cesar, a migrant worker who works on the Dover Food’s breeding farms, Blanchette explains how his managers insisted that he must either live separately from the rest of his family, or that his sibling would have to find another workplace. (Blanchette, 2015, 643) Thus, the fragility of exponentially expanding production means that producers must continuously extend the limits of their reach, even if it means altering human sociality and autonomy. Porcine life reaches out of their jobs, into their private spheres and into their psychological existence. Modern agricultural production creates fantasies and ultimately, realities of a fundamentally ahuman dimension. Producers utilize biosecurity as a warrant for control and resultantly reorder the rank of the species to prioritize industrial pork. This inverse anthropocentric relationship almost automates the hierarchy and alienation that is necessary for capitalists to continue destroying and creating– no longer do producers need to do the work of dominating, as they can control their workers with the threat of an external danger to production. They apply and execute an abstract moral imperative, one that operates, controls, and manages their lives as simply a part of the larger production process.

To return to Saito, this inversion of human and animal realms also marks an extreme mode of alienation of man from species-being. Marx defines species-being as the unique human teleological relation to nature and the human ability to modify nature to create wholly new environments. (Saito, 2017, 30). This human activity is framed in direct contrast to the animal world, wherein animals are trapped in their surroundings and can only work and consume in response to their immediate needs and environment. Yet, this description now seems apt to describe the migrant workers of Blanchette’s factory farms. This new porcine proletariat only furthers the blurring of labor politics and its boundaries. Humans and nature unfold as a growing threat to the capitalist control over people and the planet, and its own exploitations further reveal its futile attempts to control a post-anthropocentric crisis of its own doing.
